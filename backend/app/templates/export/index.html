Why this was recovered from public sources alone

Nothing in this archive came from anyone who worked on {{ project.domain }}. No private backup was consulted, no participant was asked for their files, and no material was accepted that a stranger could not obtain today. That restriction was chosen, not forced, and it is the point of the exercise: the question is not whether this site can be reconstructed by someone with access to the people who made it, but what the public record preserves on its own, for the far larger number of projects where no one kept anything and no one is left to ask.

A backup and a preserved archive are not the same object. A backup exists so that its owner can restore their own system; it is private, it assumes the owner is present to use it, and it dies with them, or with the disk, or with the last piece of software able to read its format. Preservation exists so that someone else — later, unknown, unable to ask anyone anything — can still read the material and have grounds to trust it. That is why this archive records where every file came from, when it was captured, what was looked for and not found, and a checksum for every byte. Those are not conveniences. They are what separates evidence from a copy.

The measurement that makes the case is here in the numbers. The Internet Archive was not inattentive to this site: it captured something in {{ coverage.months_seen }} of the {{ coverage.months_span }} months the studied period spans{% if coverage.missing_months %} — only {{ coverage.missing_months|length }} month{{ 's' if coverage.missing_months|length != 1 }} ({{ coverage.missing_months|map('fmt_month')|join(', ') }}) went unvisited{% endif %}. It called nearly every month for years. And still {{ counts.articles_lost_pct }}% of the articles the site linked to were never captured at all.

Presence is not coverage. A crawler saves what is on the front page the day it arrives; everything published and superseded between visits falls through, and on a working publication that is most of what gets published. Passive capture — the best the open web does by default, and the thing most people mean when they assume "it's on the Internet Archive" — loses the majority of a small site while appearing to watch it continuously. Anything more than that has to be done deliberately, by someone who decided to.

Digital and physical archives differ in what they are made of, not in what they are for. A box of papers and a directory of captures are both evidence: incomplete, shaped by what somebody chose to keep, and readable only through the record of how they came to be held. The habits that make a physical archive usable — provenance, fixity, an honest account of the gaps — are the same habits a digital one needs, and they are not automatic. This archive tries to observe them, and states plainly where it could not.

How to cite

Cite the Wayback Machine capture, not this archive’s copy: every page and link here carries its web.archive.org/web/<timestamp>/<url> address. The raw files in captures/ are byte-for-byte what the Archive served; manifest-sha256.txt lets you verify them.
